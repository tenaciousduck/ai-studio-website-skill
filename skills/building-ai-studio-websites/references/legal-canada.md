# Canada notes

Not legal advice. Last checked 2026-11-01. Saskatchewan's consumer law and federal guidance were read directly. Other provinces' consumer laws and Quebec's privacy law weren't. Points marked "from search" come from search results, not official pages.

## Email: CASL

Canada's anti-spam law covers marketing emails and text messages when a computer system in Canada is used to send or access them (s.12). So a seller elsewhere who mails Canadians is generally caught too.

- **Express consent:** a ticked box with clear wording. It lasts until the person unsubscribes.
- **Implied by purchase:** 2 years from the purchase. Set an expiry and remove the marketing tag when it passes, unless the basis has become express.
- **Implied by a subscription, membership or account:** the 2 years starts when the relationship ends, not when it began (s.10(14)). Don't expire a current member's consent.
- **An inquiry:** the law allows six months. This skill doesn't add a marketing tag for it, and offers an optional box instead.
- Every marketing email needs the sender's identity, a mailing address and an unsubscribe.

## Privacy: PIPEDA

A business collecting customer data generally falls under PIPEDA, Canada's private-sector privacy law. Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have their own laws (from memory), so check the client's province.

- Be open: a privacy contact, how to complain to the Privacy Commissioner, and what's collected and why.
- Data handled outside Canada: say so, including that authorities there may reach it.
- The business stays responsible for data its helpers handle. The Commissioner expects a contract or similar protection with service providers.
- Keep a record of any data breach for at least two years, and report serious ones.
- For a first-party visit cookie, the bigger risk found was a false promise that it was off, more than the cookie itself (judgment call).

## Selling: Saskatchewan as the example

Other provinces have similar rules with different details, so read the client's own.

- Goods are sold with warranties of acceptable quality, and a contract can't remove them (Consumer Protection and Business Practices Act, s.15, s.19). A signed or custom item can reasonably have no change-of-mind refund, but a damaged or wrong item still needs a remedy.
- The internet sales rules exclude contracts of $50 or less (Regulations, 3-2). Check the total with tax, because tax can push it over.
- Where those rules apply, the seller must show its name, business name, phone and address, a description, itemized price and total, payment terms, delivery date, carrier and policies. A copy must reach the buyer within 15 days.
- Where they apply, a buyer can cancel if goods are 30 days past the stated delivery date, with the refund due in 15 days.
- Meet these rules even under $50: Stripe's website checklist expects most of the same details.

## Subscriptions and memberships

Provinces may regulate recurring and membership contracts, including gym memberships, with their own cancellation rules. These weren't read, so check the client's province before writing terms.

## Quebec

Quebec's consumer and language rules are stricter, and contracts generally need a French version first (from search). Ask each client: exclude Quebec, add French, or knowingly accept the risk.

## Claims

The Competition Act covers advertising claims, including limited stock. Keep "only N left" wording manual and true. An all-in price with shipping included looked fine.

## Tax

GST registration is generally optional under $30,000 in sales (from search), and the client's accountant confirms it. If the seller isn't registered, say "No tax is added", and confirm the checkout agrees.

## Shipping to the US

"Shipping included" doesn't cover duties, so say who pays. Canada Post says goods sold can't go by letter post, and duties are prepaid before a parcel crosses the border. The seller may pay up front whatever the terms say, so settle the carrier before the first US order. If the client sells into the US, also read `legal-us.md`.
